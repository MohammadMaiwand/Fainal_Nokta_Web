[Heading 2] CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY
[Normal] This chapter explains the research and development methods used to design and implement NAMS. Its organization follows a rigorous software-engineering thesis progression: research methodology; requirements elicitation; population and sampling; data collection and analysis; functional, non-functional, and data requirements; development methodology; prototype iterations; system design; architecture and database design; implementation; testing; and challenges and solutions. Research methodology and development methodology are deliberately separated because they answer different questions: the former explains how evidence was gathered, while the latter explains how the software artifact was engineered.
[Normal] NAMS addresses a multi-domain management problem. Academic records, attendance, examinations, finance, communication, and reporting are not independent. A methodological approach that studies only the interface would therefore be insufficient. The project uses evidence triangulation and iterative development so that broad stakeholder needs can be converted into traceable, testable system behavior.
[Heading 3] 3.1 Research Methodology
[Normal] The research component used an applied, requirements-oriented mixed-method approach. Quantitative evidence was used to summarize organizational acceptance and structured responses, while qualitative evidence was used to interpret workflow problems and adoption barriers. The purpose was to produce defensible requirements for a real academy management system rather than to claim nationwide statistical generalization.
[Normal] The unit of analysis was the academy as an organization. Respondents were expected to have operational knowledge of registration, teaching, finance, reporting, or management. The study therefore focused on institutional workflows and readiness. Survey evidence was combined with workflow analysis, role analysis, proposal review, and prototype feedback.
[Heading 3] 3.1.1 Research Design
[Normal] A cross-sectional design was appropriate because requirements had to be captured during the system-development period. The questionnaire was structured around current practices, academic administration, financial management, communication, reporting, technology readiness, and acceptance of an integrated platform.
[Normal] The design incorporated both closed and open items. Closed questions support frequencies and percentages. Open responses provide explanations for barriers that cannot be represented adequately by a single numeric choice. This combination is especially useful when a system may be perceived as valuable but adoption is constrained by infrastructure or staffing.
[Heading 3] 3.1.2 Requirements Elicitation
[Normal] Requirements were elicited through five complementary sources: the approved project direction and Chapters 1–2; a structured academy survey; analysis of current and desired workflows; stakeholder and role analysis; and iterative prototype review. A requirement was considered stronger when multiple sources supported it.
[Normal] For example, financial transparency was not translated into a single generic 'finance module.' It was decomposed into fee obligations, payment transactions, remaining balances, debt status, income, expenses, payroll, government tax, net salary, permissions, and reports. Similarly, examination management was decomposed into online and in-person modes, question types, validation, results, and printing.
[Heading 3] 3.1.3 Population and Sampling
[Normal] The accessible organizational population consisted of approximately 20 academies considered relevant to the project context. Because the accessible population was small, the preferred approach was to approach all 20 rather than draw a substantially smaller sample. This is effectively a census of the accessible group for requirements purposes.
[Normal] Cochran’s initial sample-size formula is n₀ = Z²p(1−p)/e². For a finite population, the corrected sample is n = n₀/[1 + (n₀−1)/N]. The formula is presented to demonstrate sampling knowledge; however, when N is only 20 and the full accessible group can be approached, surveying all accessible organizations provides more direct requirements evidence. The result should therefore be described as evidence from the accessible group, not as a statistically representative estimate for all Afghan academies.
[Heading 3] 3.1.4 Data Collection Procedure
[Normal] Data collection began by mapping the research questions and objectives to information needs. Questionnaire items were then written around recognizable academy tasks. Respondents were asked about current management conditions, perceived usefulness of integrated modules, and readiness constraints. Responses were checked for completeness and organized for descriptive analysis.
[Normal] Non-survey evidence was recorded in parallel. Workflow analysis identified where the same information was entered repeatedly or reconciled manually. Role analysis identified who should access each type of data. Prototype review recorded gaps that became requirements in subsequent iterations. This combined evidence improved traceability.
[Heading 3] 3.1.5 Data Analysis Method
[Normal] Descriptive statistics were selected as the primary quantitative analysis method. Frequencies and percentages summarize categorical responses. Likert-type ratings can be summarized using distributions and, where justified, means or weighted rankings when raw data exist. No additional numerical result should be inserted without the corresponding dataset.
[Normal] Qualitative comments were grouped into practical themes such as operational efficiency, financial transparency, reporting, communication, staff capability, and technology readiness. Thematic grouping was used to explain why some organizations might support the system concept but remain unable to adopt it immediately.

[Normal] The survey should avoid unnecessary collection of personal identifiers. Organizational responses should be reported in aggregate unless explicit permission exists to identify an institution. The thesis should distinguish public information from private academic and financial information. Within NAMS, privacy principles are translated into access control, role separation, and the explicit distinction between public announcements and user-specific alerts.

[Heading 3] 3.1.10 Data Requirements
[Normal] NAMS requires persistent records for identity, roles, students, parents, teachers, managers, branches, classes, subjects, enrollments, timetables, attendance, examinations, questions, answers, results, announcements, notifications, fee obligations, payments, debts, income, expenses, payroll, tax, and reports. Referential integrity must be maintained through identifiers, validation, and controlled service logic.
[Normal] Financial data should preserve transaction history rather than relying on editable summary numbers. A student’s outstanding balance is conceptually derived from obligations minus valid payments. Payroll should preserve gross salary, applicable deductions/tax, net salary, calculation period, and rule metadata. These principles support auditability and consistent reporting.
[Heading 3] 3.1.11 Requirements Validation and Prioritization
[Normal] Requirements were reviewed for necessity, clarity, feasibility, consistency, and testability. A MoSCoW-style approach was used to distinguish Must-Have operational capabilities from later enhancements. Core identity, students, classes, attendance, examinations, payments, debt, payroll, tax, and reports were treated as high priority because other dashboards and analytics depend on trustworthy source data.
[Normal] Advanced AI prediction is intentionally classified as future-oriented unless a validated model and sufficient historical dataset are available. This prevents the thesis from overstating AI capability while preserving the project’s analytics direction.
[Heading 2] 3.2 Development Methodology
[Normal] Development followed an Agile methodology with lightweight Scrum organization, combined with explicit SDLC activities. This mirrors the useful development logic of the Exit 360 reference while adapting it to the much broader NAMS domain. Iterative work allowed missing business rules to be corrected without discarding completed modules.
[Heading 3] 3.2.1 Agile Development Approach
[Normal] The backlog was organized around coherent domains and dependencies. Authentication and permissions preceded protected modules. Student/class/enrollment foundations preceded attendance and fee obligations. Examination definitions preceded results and printing. Financial foundations preceded debt, payroll, tax, and consolidated reports. Each iteration included implementation, integration, testing, review, and documentation.

[Heading 3] 3.2.2 Scrum-Based Organization
[Normal] A lightweight Scrum model was appropriate for a small capstone team. Product-backlog items represented features, defects, technical tasks, tests, and documentation. Sprint-style iterations focused on a manageable subset of work. Supervisor and stakeholder review informed backlog reprioritization. Definition-of-done criteria included functional behavior, backend integration, permission checks, basic testing, and documentation for high-risk modules.

[Normal] Three major prototypes document the evolution of NAMS. The prototypes are functional milestones rather than merely visual mockups. Each stage has a problem statement, implemented scope, review finding, and resulting change.

[Heading 3] 3.2.4 Prototype 1 — Core Integrated Baseline
[Normal] Prototype 1 established authentication, role dashboards, students, teachers, classes, attendance, examinations, results, payments, and initial financial views. The purpose was to verify that the principal academy domains could operate in one connected platform.
[Normal] The review identified a financial gap: government salary tax was not explicitly represented in the payroll flow. Without this element, gross salary and net payable salary could not be documented transparently. The gap was therefore converted into a formal requirement for Prototype 2.

[Heading 3] 3.2.5 Prototype 2 — Government Tax and Financial Correction
[Normal] Prototype 2 refined the financial subsystem. Payroll was redesigned to distinguish gross salary, applicable tax or deductions, and net salary. Financial reports were reorganized so that authorized users could understand the origin of totals instead of viewing disconnected values.
[Normal] The exact tax formula must be based on an authoritative and current Afghan legal source. The implementation should keep tax rules configurable or versioned so historical payroll can remain reproducible when rules change.
[Normal] Prototype 2 review revealed the need for explicit debt management. Unpaid balances should not be inferred manually from separate screens. This finding motivated Prototype 3.

[Heading 3] 3.2.6 Prototype 3 — Debt and Printing Enhancements
[Normal] Prototype 3 introduced explicit student debt management by connecting fee obligations to payment transactions and remaining balances. Authorized users can identify unpaid or partially paid obligations and review payment history.
[Normal] The prototype also introduced class-specific attendance printing. A printable sheet is generated from system data and can include academy, class, subject, teacher, date, student roster, attendance columns, and signature areas.
[Normal] The examination module was extended so authorized users can print question papers directly from stored exams. Descriptive, multiple-choice, and true/false questions are rendered according to their type. An optional answer key can be restricted to authorized staff.

[Normal] System design converts validated requirements into complementary behavioral, structural, architectural, and data models. The artifact sequence follows recognized software-engineering practice: use case model, sequence diagrams, data-flow models, architecture, database design, class model, and deployment view. Each diagram below is specific to NAMS.
[Heading 3] 3.3.1 Use Case Model
[Normal] The use case model places NAMS within a system boundary and keeps external actors outside it. Actors include administrators, teachers, students, parents, managers, and finance-authorized users. The model shows major responsibilities but does not replace detailed RBAC rules.

[Heading 3] 3.3.4 Architectural Design
[Normal] The architecture separates presentation, API/security, domain services, and persistence/infrastructure. React web and Flutter mobile clients share backend rules through authenticated APIs. Cross-cutting middleware handles identity, authorization, validation, and audit concerns. Domain services encapsulate business rules so calculations are not duplicated across clients.

[Normal] The architecture supports maintainability and scalability. A future notification provider, PDF generator, or analytics service can be replaced behind an adapter without rewriting core academic logic. Similarly, mobile and web clients can evolve independently while relying on the same authoritative backend calculations.
[Heading 3] 3.3.5 Database Design
[Normal] The conceptual ERD represents relationships among core academic and financial entities. Although MongoDB is document-oriented, conceptual cardinalities remain important for data integrity and reporting.

[Normal] Students and classes are connected through enrollments. Fee obligations belong to enrollment context and receive payment transactions. Exams contain questions and produce results. Teachers receive payroll records that may have tax records. These relationships prevent disconnected totals and support traceable reports.

[Heading 2] 3.4 Implementation
[Normal] Implementation translated the design into a modular full-stack system using Node.js and Express.js, MongoDB, React, and Flutter-oriented mobile components. The implementation was organized by domain to keep authentication, academic workflows, finance, communication, and reporting maintainable.
[Heading 3] 3.4.1 Development Environment
[Normal] The development environment includes Node.js tooling, package management, MongoDB, React development tools, Flutter/Dart SDK, Android tooling for physical-device testing, Git/GitHub, browser developer tools, and API-testing utilities. Environment variables should separate local, testing, and production configuration.
[Heading 3] 3.4.2 Backend Implementation
[Normal] Express routes receive requests and delegate to middleware and domain services. Authentication verifies identity; authorization verifies permission; validation checks request data; services implement business rules; persistence code reads and writes MongoDB. Centralized error handling should return consistent responses without leaking sensitive internal details.
[Heading 3] 3.4.3 Web Frontend Implementation
[Normal] The React web application supports administration, finance, reports, and other desktop-oriented workflows. Reusable components, consistent form validation, protected routes, loading/error states, and responsive layout improve maintainability and usability.
[Heading 3] 3.4.4 Mobile Application Implementation
[Normal] The Flutter mobile application provides role-specific access for supported users. Mobile design must account for smaller screens, RTL/LTR direction, intermittent connectivity, physical-device API access, and clear separation of public and authenticated experiences.
[Heading 3] 3.4.5 Authentication and RBAC
[Normal] Security depends on backend enforcement. A hidden button is not an authorization control. Protected API operations must verify authenticated identity and required permission. Role definitions should be centralized and tested against unauthorized requests.
[Heading 3] 3.4.6 Academic Modules
[Normal] Student, teacher, class, subject, enrollment, timetable, and attendance modules form the academic foundation. Their identifiers and relationships are reused by examinations, results, payments, and reports.
[Heading 3] 3.4.7 Examination Module
[Normal] The examination subsystem supports online and in-person workflows. In-person exams use typed questions: descriptive questions store a prompt; MCQs store options and a correct answer when grading requires it; true/false questions restrict options to the logical pair. Printing uses stored content.
[Heading 3] 3.4.8 Financial Module
[Normal] The financial subsystem separates obligations, transactions, debt, income, expenses, payroll, tax, and reports. Remaining debt is derived from obligations and valid payments. Payroll preserves calculation inputs and outputs. Sensitive operations require finance/management permissions.
[Heading 3] 3.4.9 Communication Module
[Normal] Public announcements and private alerts are stored and retrieved through different access paths. Public pages may show published academy notices, but personal payment, account, or academic alerts must be limited to the intended authenticated user or authorized related role.
[Heading 3] 3.4.10 Reporting and Printing
[Normal] Reports should be generated from authoritative source records. Printable attendance and examination documents use consistent templates. Financial reports should identify period, filters, totals, and underlying categories. Exported documents should include sufficient context to remain understandable outside the application.
[Heading 3] 3.4.11 Analytics and AI Readiness
[Normal] NAMS prepares structured data for future analytics. Attendance trends, examination results, enrollment, and operational indicators can support later analysis. Predictive AI should not be claimed as validated until a sufficient dataset, evaluation protocol, privacy controls, and human oversight are available.

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 5: DISCUSSION
[Normal] This chapter interprets the survey and engineering results in relation to the research questions, objectives, literature themes, and limitations. The discussion distinguishes implemented capability from empirically proven long-term impact.
[Heading 2] 5.1 Interpretation of Main Findings
[Normal] The findings indicate that academy management is a connected information problem. Enrollment affects class membership and fee obligations; attendance and exams depend on class membership; results support monitoring; payments determine debt; teacher assignments affect payroll; and all domains contribute to reporting. Integration therefore creates value through consistency across workflows.
[Normal] The 85% support result suggests meaningful interest within the accessible group. The 15% non-readiness result is equally informative because it identifies facilitating conditions—computers and suitable staff—as adoption constraints. A deployment plan should therefore include onboarding and readiness assessment.
[Heading 2] 5.2 Discussion of Financial Transparency
[Normal] Financial transparency is a distinguishing requirement of NAMS. A generic learning platform may manage courses without modeling the academy’s operational finances. NAMS connects fee obligations, payment transactions, remaining debt, expenses, payroll, government tax, and reports.
[Normal] The prototype history shows that financial completeness evolved iteratively. Government tax was a missing requirement in Prototype 1; Prototype 2 corrected payroll visibility; Prototype 3 added explicit debt. This progression supports the argument that prototype review can reveal business rules that high-level requirements overlook.
[Heading 2] 5.3 Discussion of Academic and Examination Workflows
[Normal] Attendance and examinations are linked to the class/enrollment model so that records remain contextual. The internal exam builder supports descriptive, multiple-choice, and true/false questions. Printing from stored questions reduces duplicate preparation and preserves consistency between digital and paper artifacts.
[Normal] Future evaluation should measure whether these workflows actually reduce staff time or errors. The present monograph can demonstrate implemented capability but should avoid claiming measured efficiency gains without before-and-after data.
[Heading 2] 5.4 Communication, Privacy, and RBAC
[Normal] Public announcements and personal alerts have different privacy expectations. The system design separates them so public pages display academy notices rather than user-specific warnings. RBAC further restricts sensitive operations such as payroll, tax, debt, and role management.
[Normal] Security must be enforced at the API layer. Interface hiding improves usability but cannot prevent direct unauthorized requests. This principle is reflected in the security testing plan.
[Heading 2] 5.5 AI and Learning Analytics Direction
[Normal] The project’s AI direction should be interpreted as an analytics-ready foundation. Structured attendance, results, enrollment, and learning records can later support risk detection or recommendations. However, responsible predictive analytics requires sufficient historical data, model validation, fairness analysis, privacy protection, and human oversight.
[Normal] This distinction strengthens academic credibility because it separates future potential from demonstrated results.
[Heading 2] 5.6 Adoption and Local Context
[Normal] The three non-ready academies demonstrate that local context affects deployment. Hardware availability, staff digital skills, connectivity, training, and organizational routines can determine whether a system is used successfully. Mobile access may reduce some barriers but cannot replace administrative capacity.
[Normal] A phased pilot at Nokta Academy followed by broader academy deployment would provide stronger evidence than immediate large-scale rollout.

[Heading 2] 6.4 Recommendations
[Normal] A controlled pilot should be conducted at Nokta Academy before wider deployment. The pilot should measure task completion time, reporting accuracy, payment reconciliation, debt follow-up, user satisfaction, and support needs. Staff training and minimum hardware guidance should be prepared for institutions with readiness constraints.

[Normal] Future enhancements may include stronger offline synchronization, push notifications, configurable billing plans, branch comparison, advanced report templates, inventory/document management where justified, and expanded parent services. Feature expansion should preserve modular architecture and permission boundaries.
[Normal] AI features should be developed only after sufficient clean historical data exist. Candidate directions include attendance-risk detection, performance trend analysis, learning-resource recommendations, and management summaries. Models should be evaluated for accuracy, bias, explainability, privacy, and appropriate human review.
[Heading 2] 6.6 Final Conclusion
[Normal] NAMS demonstrates a coherent approach to integrating academic administration, finance, communication, reporting, and operational printing within one academy management platform. The project’s strongest contribution is not the number of screens but the relationships between workflows: enrollment connects to fees, payments connect to debt, teacher work connects to payroll, examinations connect to results and printing, and role permissions govern access throughout.
[Normal] The project also demonstrates the importance of iterative refinement. The progression from the Prototype 1 tax gap to Prototype 2 financial correction and Prototype 3 debt/printing enhancements shows how a capstone system can mature through structured feedback. With further production testing, broader user evaluation, verified financial rules, and responsible analytics development, NAMS can provide a strong foundation for digital transformation in private academy management.
