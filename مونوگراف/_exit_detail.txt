CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY
This chapter explains the research and development methods used to design
and implement Exit 360. It describes the quantitative questionnaire-based
requirements elicitation process, the target population and sampling approach,
data collection and analysis, system requirements, and the main development
methodology followed during the project. It also presents the system design,
architecture, database design, implementation approach, and testing activities
that connect the identified preparation needs with the final mobile application.
3.1
Research Methodology
This study used a quantitative research methodology to support the requirements
elicitation for Exit 360. The main data collection instrument was a structured
questionnaire administered to medical graduates and final-year students
preparing for the Afghan Medical Exit Exam. It was used to measure candidates’
preparation habits, access challenges, internet availability, feature priorities,
and expected usefulness of an Exit Exam preparation mobile application.
Before preparing the questionnaire, an initial feature set was derived from the
literature review and analysis of existing medical exam preparation systems both
local and foreign. The reviewed literature helped identify the educational value
of repeated practice, timed tests, feedback, and progress monitoring, while the
existing-system review showed how local and international preparation apps
support question banks, mock tests, answer review, offline access, analytics, and
online learning features. These sources were used to create a preliminary list of
features for Exit 360, which was then presented to the target users through the
questionnaire.
The quantitative questionnaire responses were analyzed using descriptive
statistics such as frequencies, percentages, mean scores, and priority rankings.
This made it possible to summarize the main preparation problems faced by Exit
Exam candidates and to identify which proposed features were considered most
useful. The findings were then used to refine the functional and non-functional
requirements of Exit 360, especially in areas such as offline question practice,
timed mock tests, answer review, progress tracking, synchronization, and
optional online competition.
3.1.1
Requirements Elicitation
Requirements elicitation began by translating the problem context into a practical
feature list for Exit 360. The literature review showed that Exit Exam candidates

need repeated practice, exam-like testing, feedback, and support for broad
subject revision. The review of local and foreign medical exam preparation
applications then helped identify common system patterns, such as question
banks, timed mock tests, answer review, progress tracking, offline content, and
online learning activities. These sources provided the starting point for defining
what an Exit Exam preparation application should support.
Based on this initial analysis, a preliminary set of proposed features was
prepared for user validation. The proposed features included offline subject-wise
practice, general practice tests, timed test sessions, automatic grading, answer
review, attempt history, performance statistics, authenticated online profiles,
synchronization, weekly tests, leaderboards, and challenge tests. The purpose
of preparing this feature list was to make the requirements concrete enough
for respondents to evaluate, rather than asking only general questions about
whether a preparation application would be useful.
A structured questionnaire was then used to collect feedback from the target
users.
The questionnaire measured preparation habits, access to practice
tests, internet availability, previous use of preparation applications, perceived
usefulness of proposed features, feature priority rankings, and willingness to
use an offline-capable Exit Exam preparation app. These responses helped
determine which requirements were most important for the first version of Exit
360.
The final requirements were therefore shaped by both literature-based feature
identification and quantitative user feedback. This helped keep the application
focused on real preparation needs, including offline access, organized question
practice, timed mock testing, review, progress awareness, and optional online
competition when internet access is available.
3.1.2
Population and Sampling
The target population of this study consisted of individuals directly relevant to
Afghan Medical Exit Exam preparation. This included curative medicine and
stomatology graduates, as well as final-year curative medicine and stomatology
students who were approaching graduation and preparing for the licensing
exam. This population was selected because Exit 360 was intended for medical
graduates and senior students who need support in subject-wise revision, practice
testing, answer review, progress tracking, offline study, and optional online
competition.
Although a broader population of all curative medicine and stomatology students

3.1.3
Data Collection
Data for the user requirements part of the study were collected through a
structured questionnaire. The questionnaire was selected because it allowed
us to collect comparable responses from a group of respondents who were
directly relevant to Exit Exam preparation within the limited project timeline. It
also matched the purpose of this part of the research, which was to understand
candidates’ preparation conditions, their access to practice tests and internet,
and their level of interest in the proposed features of Exit 360.
Two versions of the questionnaire were prepared. The English version was
prepared for thesis documentation, while the Persian version was prepared
for actual respondents so that the questions would be easier to understand
and answer. Data collection was carried out through printed Persian copies
distributed in person to eligible respondents. This approach was selected
because the target respondents were more reachable through direct distribution,
and it reduced the need for respondents to depend on an internet connection
while completing the survey.
The questionnaire was titled Exit 360 Survey Questionnaire. It began with a short
introduction explaining that the survey was part of a Kabul University Software
Engineering capstone project for developing an Exit Exam preparation mobile
application. The introduction also informed respondents that participation was
voluntary, that the survey was anonymous, and that no personal identifying
information would be published.
The questionnaire contained 28 items organized into five sections. The first
section, Demographics, collected basic background information about the
respondents. It asked about age, gender, original province, current study level,
whether the respondent had already taken the Afghan Medical Exit Exam,
and whether the respondent was attending an Exit Exam preparation course
or coaching program. These items helped describe the respondent group and
provided context for interpreting their answers.
The second section, Current Practice and Access, focused on the respondents’
existing preparation habits and access conditions. It included questions about
how often respondents take mock or practice tests, how easy it is for them to

access practice tests on demand, how they currently prepare for the Exit Exam,
how often they have internet access for studying, how reliable their internet
connection is, how important offline access is to them, and whether they have
used any Exit Exam or medical exam preparation app before. This section was
included because Exit 360 was intended to respond to real preparation barriers,
especially access to practice questions, internet limitations, and the usefulness
of offline support.
The third section, Feature Interest and Priorities, asked respondents to
evaluate the planned features of Exit 360. It presented six proposed features:
offline practice tests, performance analytics dashboard, subject-wise weak-area
tracking, user profiles and peer following, online weekly competitive tests
and leaderboards, and peer-to-peer challenge mode. Respondents rated the
usefulness of each feature on a five-point scale from Not at all useful to
Extremely useful. They were also asked to select and rank their top three
most important features, and to indicate how likely they would be to use an
Exit Exam preparation mobile application with these features. This section
directly supported requirements prioritization because it showed which proposed
features were most valued by potential users.
The fourth section, Perceived Impact, measured respondents’ agreement with
statements about the expected benefits of the application. The statements asked
whether the app would improve exam preparation, help students practice more
frequently, help identify strengths and weaknesses, help them focus on subjects
where they need more practice, motivate more consistent study, increase exam
confidence, and make it easier to study anywhere and anytime. These items
used a five-point agreement scale from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree.
This section helped capture the perceived educational value of the proposed
system, not only interest in individual features.
The fifth section, Experience and Open Feedback, included open-ended
questions.
These questions asked respondents about the biggest barriers
they face in preparing for the Exit Exam, what they liked and disliked about any
Exit Exam or medical exam preparation apps they had used, what features would
make them choose an exam preparation app over paper-based or center-based
methods, and any additional comments or suggestions regarding an exam
preparation platform. These open questions were included to allow respondents
to express issues or ideas that might not be fully captured by fixed-choice
questions.
Overall, the questionnaire combined multiple item types, including short-
answer demographic items, multiple-choice questions, five-point usefulness

3.1.4
Data Analysis and Presentation
The closed-ended questionnaire responses were analyzed using descriptive
statistics. Since the purpose of the questionnaire was exploratory requirements
elicitation, the analysis focused on summarizing the responses clearly rather than
making nationwide statistical claims. Categorical questions, such as study level,
Exit Exam experience, preparation method, access to practice tests, internet
access, offline-access importance, and prior use of Exit Exam or medical exam
preparation apps, were summarized using frequencies and percentages.
For the five-point rating items, the analysis used mean and median scores to show
the overall level of support for each proposed feature and each perceived-impact
statement. In addition, responses were grouped where useful. For example,
Very useful and Extremely useful responses were combined to show strong
feature interest, while Agree and Strongly Agree responses were combined to
show positive perceived impact.
The top-three feature priority question was analyzed using a weighted scoring
method. A first-rank feature was assigned three points, a second-rank feature
was assigned two points, and a third-rank feature was assigned one point. This
made it possible to compare not only how often features were mentioned, but
also how highly respondents prioritized them.
The results were presented through tables and charts generated from the
questionnaire dataset. The charts were produced using Python from the original
CSV file so that the analysis could be regenerated if the data changed. The
results were organized in Chapter 4 according to the main questionnaire sections,
including respondent profile, current practice and access, feature interest and
priorities, perceived impact, and summary findings. This structure helped
connect the survey results directly to the requirements of Exit 360.
3.1.5
Functional Requirements
The functional requirements describe the main services that Exit 360 must
provide to its users. They were derived from the problem statement, the
questionnaire findings, the review of existing Exit Exam and medical exam
preparation applications, and the planned system scope described in the project
33


===== PAGE 52 =====

documentation.
FR-001: User Account and Profile Management
The system shall allow students to create an account, sign in, and maintain a
basic profile. The profile should include information such as name, province,
study level, university or preparation background, username, and optional
profile image details. This information is needed for personalization, social
features, competitive tests, synchronization, and progress tracking.
FR-002: Offline Question Bank Access
The system shall provide access to a bundled Exit Exam question bank inside
the mobile application. Students should be able to view and attempt questions
without requiring an active internet connection. This requirement is important
because many target users have limited or unreliable internet access, and the
core preparation experience should remain useful offline.
FR-003: Subject-Wise Practice Tests
The system shall allow students to practice questions by selecting specific
subjects. Subject-wise practice should help students focus on weak areas, repeat
difficult topics, and prepare across the clinical and paraclinical subjects included
in the Exit Exam in a more organized way.
FR-004: General Mixed Practice Tests
The system shall allow students to take general mixed-subject practice tests
that resemble the structure of the Exit Exam more closely than single-subject
practice. Students should be able to configure basic test options such as the
number of questions and whether a timer is used.
FR-005: Timed Test Sessions
The system shall support timed test sessions for both offline practice and online
competitive tests. The timer should help students practice under exam-like
pressure and should record the duration of each attempt so that results can be
reviewed later.
FR-006: Automatic Grading and Result Display
The system shall automatically grade completed practice tests and display the
result to the student. The result should include the total score, number of correct
and incorrect answers, and where possible, subject-level performance. For
Exit-style tests, the result should also support percentage-based interpretation
so students can understand their performance in relation to the passing score.
34

For competitive online tests, trusted grading should be handled by the backend
rather than the mobile client.
FR-007: Attempt History and Performance Analytics
The system shall store students’ test attempts and present basic performance
analytics. These analytics should include recent scores, score trends, average
performance, stronger subjects, weaker subjects, and frequently missed questions.
This requirement supports the need for faster feedback and better progress
awareness during preparation.
FR-008: Offline Attempt Synchronization
The system shall allow attempts created offline to be stored locally and
synchronized with the cloud account when internet access becomes available.
Synchronization should avoid losing students’ practice records and should keep
local progress and cloud data reasonably consistent.
FR-009: Weekly Competitive Tests and Leaderboards
The system shall provide online weekly tests generated and controlled by the
backend. Students should be able to join available weekly tests, submit answers,
and view published leaderboard results after grading is complete. Leaderboards
should be based on official backend results so that rankings remain fair and
auditable.
FR-010: Peer Following and Challenge Tests
The system shall allow students to find and follow peers or friends, and to
participate in friends-only challenge tests. Challenge tests should support
asynchronous participation, so both students do not need to be online at the
same time. This requirement supports motivation, comparison, and peer-based
learning without making competition mandatory for all users.
FR-011: Exit Exam Blueprint Alignment
The system shall organize practice content according to the subject structure
of the Afghan Medical Exit Exam. Questions should be grouped in a way that

CHAPTER 5: DISCUSSION
This chapter discusses the meaning of the findings and development outcomes
presented in the previous chapters.
It connects the questionnaire results,
literature review, system requirements, prototype reviews, implementation work,
and testing evidence to the main aim of the project. The purpose of the chapter
was not to repeat every result from Chapter 4, but to explain what those results
suggested about the need for Exit 360 and the value of the solution within its
defined scope.
5.1
Main Findings
The findings of this project supported the idea that Exit Exam preparation
required a more structured and accessible preparation tool.
The problem
identified in the earlier chapters was not simply that candidates needed more
reading material. The broader issue was that preparation for the Afghan medical
Exit Exam involved many subjects, timed performance, repeated practice, review
of mistakes, and awareness of progress. The questionnaire results in Chapter 4
therefore mattered because they helped translate this general problem into
practical system requirements.
One important finding was the need for more flexible access to practice. The
questionnaire results showed that respondents did not always have convenient
access to mock tests and regular practice opportunities. This supported the
decision to make practice tests available inside the mobile application rather than
tying preparation only to physical centers, printed material, or fixed schedules.
For a licensing exam that covers a broad subject area, such flexibility was
important because candidates needed repeated opportunities to practice, review,
and return to weak subjects.
The results also supported the offline-capable direction of the system. Internet
access and reliability were important concerns in the Afghan context, and this
matched the background evidence discussed in Chapter 1. As a result, the
project treated offline preparation as the foundation of the system rather than
as an optional extra. The local question bank, SQLite-based attempt storage,
offline test creation, local grading, history, and statistics were therefore central
to the project. The online layer was designed to add value through accounts,
weekly tests, leaderboards, and challenges when connectivity was available, but
the basic preparation workflow did not depend on a constant internet connection.
Another finding was that users valued feedback and progress awareness. A
preparation system that only showed whether a single test was passed or failed
97

would not be enough for long-term study. Exit 360 therefore included answer
review, attempt history, basic statistics, subject-level performance summaries,
and weak-area awareness. These features helped turn repeated practice into
a more meaningful study process because users could see where they had
performed well and where they needed more revision.
The findings also suggested that online competition could be useful if it remained
secondary to the core preparation experience. Weekly tests, leaderboards, and
peer challenges were relevant because they could encourage regular practice
and give students a sense of comparison. However, the project did not treat
competition as a replacement for study. Instead, it was used as an additional
motivational layer. This was important because the local context made it
unrealistic to build a system that required every candidate to be online at all
times.
5.2
Relation to Objectives and Research Questions
The main objective of the project was to design and implement an Afghan medical
Exit Exam preparation mobile application. This objective was addressed through
the development of Exit 360, which combined a Flutter mobile application, a
local SQLite question bank, Firebase-backed online services, and an internal
web admin panel. The main research question asked how such an application
could be designed and implemented. The project answered this by using a
mobile-first and offline-capable architecture, then adding online features through
controlled backend services.
The first sub objective was to identify the existing challenges faced by Exit
Exam candidates and analyze their main preparation needs. This was addressed
through the background study, literature review, review of existing systems, and
questionnaire. Together, these sources showed that candidates needed organized
subject-wise practice, timed mock tests, answer review, progress tracking, and
offline access. The results also showed that online activities could be useful,
but only when they complemented the offline preparation workflow.
The second sub objective was to design and implement an Exit Exam preparation
mobile application based on the identified requirements. This was addressed by
translating the findings into functional, non-functional, and data requirements.
The mobile application implemented offline practice, configurable tests, result
display, review, attempt history, and statistics. The online side implemented
authenticated accounts, weekly tests, leaderboards, friends, and asynchronous
challenges. The admin panel supported operational work such as inspecting
platform activity, managing weekly-test workflows, reviewing question-bank

summaries, and publishing leaderboards.
The third sub objective was to test and evaluate the developed system in
relation to its intended objectives. This was addressed through automated
tests, integration testing, emulator-based checks, deployed Firebase testing, and
prototype reviews. The prototype reviews were especially important because
they connected implementation work with user and developer feedback. The
first prototype reviewed the offline preparation flow, the second reviewed the
online social and competition layer, and the third reviewed the internal admin
workflow. These reviews did not prove long-term educational effectiveness, but
they helped evaluate whether the system direction and implemented workflows
matched the intended objectives.
5.3
Relation to Literature and Existing Systems
The discussion of Exit 360 was also connected to the literature and existing
systems reviewed in Chapter 2. The educational literature supported the value
of repeated testing and feedback. As emphasized by Parker (2020), repeated
low-stakes testing has learning value, while according to Zhang et al. (2023),
realistic practice exams could help learners recognize gaps and prepare more
actively. These ideas were reflected in Exit 360 through subject-wise tests,
general practice tests, timed sessions, automatic grading, and review of previous
attempts.
The mobile learning literature also supported the project direction. According
to R¨uth et al. (2021), mobile quiz tools could support flexible self-regulated
learning, and according to Lazarinis et al. (2017), mobile self-assessment allowed
students to practice and receive feedback outside formal classroom settings.
This was relevant to Exit 360 because many candidates needed preparation
support that could fit into different study times and access conditions. A mobile
application was therefore a suitable form for the proposed solution.
The review of local systems showed that Afghan medical exam preparation
applications were already emerging, but the landscape was still incomplete.
MedQuiz showed local interest in digital medical exam preparation, but its
online-first and paid access model limited what users could evaluate freely
(Azmoonak Tebi, 2026). EXIT Medical Exam Prep provided a strong offline
example with bilingual question access, timed tests, and answer review, but it
remained limited in long-term analytics and online competition (Zahid apps
store, 2026). Exit 360 therefore did not claim to be the only preparation app.
Its contribution was the attempt to combine offline access, progress awareness,
Exit-focused practice, and optional online competition in one coherent workflow.

The foreign systems reviewed in Chapter 2 showed a more mature direction
for licensing exam preparation. UWorld treated practice as a full cycle of
questions, timed blocks, explanations, assessment, and review (UWorld, 2026).
AMBOSS connected question practice with medical reference material and
performance signals (AMBOSS, 2026). SMLE Gate showed how a focused
national licensing-exam app could support simulated exams, notes, saved
questions, result review, and progress tracking (SMLE Gate, 2026). These
systems helped clarify useful design patterns, but they also showed the difference
between large commercial platforms and a student capstone project. Exit 360
adapted the practical ideas that matched the Afghan context while leaving
multimedia-heavy content, large-scale coaching, and advanced commercial
features outside the project scope.
The literature on online learning conditions in Afghanistan further supported
the offline-first approach. In the study by Gawhari et al. (2021), limitations
were identified in relation to internet access, connection speed, cost, and digital
learning materials. This matched the design decision to keep core preparation
local while treating Firebase-backed features as added value. The discussion
therefore confirmed that the technical design of Exit 360 was not only a
development preference. It was a response to the educational and infrastructural
conditions described in the earlier chapters.
5.4
Practical Implications of Exit 360
For Exit Exam candidates, the practical implication of Exit 360 was that
preparation could become more organized and repeatable. Instead of depending
only on scattered notes, printed questions, or occasional mock tests, students
could use the application to select subjects, take timed or untimed tests, review
answers, and monitor their progress. This did not replace textbooks, teachers,
or preparation centers, but it provided a structured support tool that could be
used alongside them.
The offline-first design had a direct practical value. Since the main practice
workflow was stored locally, the app could remain useful when the internet was
unavailable or unreliable. This was important for a target group that may not
always have stable access to online platforms. At the same time, the optional
online layer allowed candidates to participate in weekly tests, leaderboards, and
challenges when internet access was available. In this way, the system balanced
accessibility with motivation.
Technically, the project showed how a student team could combine local
mobile storage with cloud-backed trusted workflows.

The internal admin panel also had practical importance. It allowed controlled
operational work without requiring direct Firestore editing for every task. This
mattered because online weekly tests, leaderboard publishing, question-bank
summaries, and platform inspection require careful handling. The admin panel
therefore supported the maintainability and operational side of the system, even
though it remained within the scope of a capstone prototype.
From a Software Engineering perspective, the project demonstrated the value
of connecting requirements elicitation, system design, implementation, testing,
and documentation. The questionnaire helped identify user needs, the literature
review helped shape feature direction, the prototypes created reviewable working
stages, and the testing process helped verify important logic and integration
points. This made Exit 360 more than a simple mobile app because the software
was supported by a documented engineering process.
5.5
Limitations of the Findings and System
The discussion of Exit 360 must also be understood in light of the project’s
limitations. The questionnaire provided useful requirements-oriented evidence,
but it did not represent all Exit Exam candidates in Afghanistan. The achieved
sample size was smaller than the statistically recommended sample, and the
respondents were selected through a convenience sampling approach. Therefore,
the findings were useful for guiding system requirements and early evaluation,
but they should not be treated as a complete national measurement of all
candidates’ needs.
The content limitation was also important. The current question bank was
limited in size and was prepared in Dari. A complete bilingual Dari and
Pashto question bank would require expert medical review, careful content
authorization, and long-term maintenance. The project avoided direct question
mining from copyrighted books or unauthorized sources, which was appropriate
from an ethical and academic perspective. However, this also meant that the
current question bank could not be presented as a full replacement for official
or expert-reviewed preparation materials.

CHAPTER 5: DISCUSSION
The system was also limited by its prototype-level deployment. The project
implemented and tested important offline and online workflows, but it was
not deployed as a mature production platform with large-scale users, complete
monitoring, complete notification coverage, and full app-store release. Firebase
deployment also introduced a practical constraint because Cloud Functions
required upgrading from the free Spark plan to the Blaze plan, and Google
Cloud Billing did not support Afghan payment methods. This showed that
technical implementation can be affected by regional service and payment
limitations, not only by programming difficulty.
Another limitation was that the project did not measure long-term learning
effectiveness or official exam outcomes. The questionnaire, prototype reviews,
and tests supported the relevance, usability direction, and functional correctness
of the system, but they did not prove that using Exit 360 increased official pass
rates. Such a claim would require a longer study with a larger sample, controlled
comparison, validated question content, and follow-up after the actual Exit
Exam.
Finally, some advanced features remained outside the project scope. These
included real-time multiplayer testing, continuously updating live leaderboards,
large-scale tournaments, video lessons, medical animations, advanced adaptive
learning, and a complete production-grade content management system. These
exclusions helped keep the project realistic for the available timeline, team size,
and resources, but they also showed where future work could expand the system.
Within these limitations, the questionnaire findings, literature review, existing-
system analysis, prototype reviews, implementation work, and testing evidence
indicated that Exit 360 addressed the identified preparation need within the
project scope. It combined offline question practice, timed tests, answer review,
progress tracking, optional online competition, and controlled administrative
workflows in a locally relevant system. Exit 360 should therefore be understood
as a capstone prototype and documented software engineering project, not as a
complete national exam platform or a proven replacement for formal preparation
resources. Its contribution was in translating a real preparation problem into
a working and technically structured solution. The next chapter presents the
overall conclusion of the project and recommendations for future work.

6.4
Future Enhancements
The first important future enhancement is the expansion of the question bank.
The current question bank is limited and was prepared mainly to support the
implemented workflows. A future version should include a larger, expert-
reviewed, and properly authorized bilingual Dari and Pashto question bank.
Medical teachers and subject specialists should be involved so that questions
are accurate, properly categorized, and aligned with the Exit Exam subject
structure.
The mobile application can also be improved through stronger polish and user-
support features. Future work should improve onboarding, profile completion,
authentication messages, password recovery, email verification, notification
coverage, and overall interface consistency. The practice section can also be
improved with better review filters, improved attempt management, stronger
weak-area summaries, and clearer progress visualization.
The online competition layer should be expanded carefully. Weekly tests,
leaderboards, friends, and challenges were implemented as core online
workflows, but they can be refined further through better navigation, clearer
friend and challenge displays, richer leaderboard information, improved
notification handling, and stronger user experience around scheduled tests
and published results. These improvements should preserve the offline-first
foundation and avoid making the app dependent on constant connectivity.
The admin panel also requires future development. Important improvements
