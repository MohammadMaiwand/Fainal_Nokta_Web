[Heading 1] ABSTRACT
[Normal] Nokta Academy currently faces academic and administrative inefficiencies caused by manual records, disconnected spreadsheets, delayed reporting, limited communication, and weak financial transparency. This monograph presents the Nokta Academy Management System (NAMS), an integrated web and mobile-oriented platform designed to automate student enrollment, class and subject management, attendance, grading, notifications, fee payments, teacher payroll, tax calculation, reporting, and role-based access. The system uses a modern full-stack architecture built with Node.js, Express.js, MongoDB, React.js, and mobile-ready application components. It also prepares a structured foundation for AI-based learning analytics and student performance monitoring. The methodology combines Agile Scrum with structured SDLC phases. Requirements were gathered through questionnaire-based needs assessment, workflow analysis, role analysis, and proposal review. From a population of 100 stakeholders, 75 valid responses were selected, and 64 respondents approved the project, producing an 85.33% acceptance rate. The system was designed through architecture modeling, use case modeling, class modeling, database ERD, functional and non-functional requirement specification, and security design. The results show that NAMS can reduce manual work, improve data accuracy, centralize communication, strengthen financial accountability, and support evidence-based decision-making. The project contributes a context-aware digital transformation model for private academies in Afghanistan while following software engineering standards, APA citation practices, copyright awareness, and Mendeley-compatible reference management.

[Heading 1] CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
[Heading 2] 1.1 Background
[Normal] Digital transformation has become a central strategy for educational institutions that need accurate data, faster administration, transparent finance, and better communication. In many developing contexts, including Afghanistan, academies still depend on paper files, handwritten ledgers, or disconnected spreadsheets for enrollment, scheduling, payment tracking, grades, and attendance. These tools may appear simple at the beginning, but they become unreliable when student numbers, teacher assignments, financial transactions, and reporting obligations increase. Research on learning analytics and educational data mining shows that educational data becomes valuable when it is collected consistently and transformed into actionable information (Aldowah et al., 2019; Romero & Ventura, 2020).
[Normal] Nokta Academy Management System is designed as an integrated web and mobile-oriented platform that centralizes academic, administrative, communication, and financial operations. It supports student registration, class and subject management, attendance, exams, results, notifications, payments, teacher salary, tax calculation, academy financial reports, and dashboards. This integration responds directly to the proposal’s main idea: developing a modern online academy platform with Node.js, Express.js, MongoDB, React.js, Flutter-oriented access, and AI-ready analytics.
[Normal] The system also reflects the global movement toward smart educational systems. Smart educational platforms are not limited to online content delivery; they involve data-driven administration, user-centered design, real-time communication, and analytics-supported decision-making (Al-Samarraie & Saeed, 2022; Siemens, 2013). In NAMS, dashboard analytics, financial reporting, and student performance modules provide a foundation for future AI features such as risk detection and personalized recommendations.
[Normal] From a software engineering perspective, the project is important because it translates a proposal into a working artifact. The project uses an iterative structure, modular APIs, role-based access, MongoDB schemas, and React-based interfaces. Software engineering literature emphasizes that successful systems should be developed from clear requirements, designed with maintainable architecture, and tested against expected behavior (Pressman & Maxim, 2020; Sommerville, 2016).
[Normal] The local importance of NAMS is also visible in its financial workflows. Many academy systems handle grades and attendance, but do not calculate teacher commission salary, government salary tax, student fee balances, and academy financial reports in an integrated way. NAMS connects student payments with teacher payroll and reporting. This makes the project more than an ordinary record-keeping system; it becomes a management decision-support platform for academy operations.
[Heading 2] 1.2 Problem Statement
[Normal] Nokta Academy experiences several academic and administrative inefficiencies due to the absence of a centralized digital management system. Essential operations such as student enrollment, attendance tracking, grading, timetable management, salary calculation, fee collection, and reporting are difficult to control when they are handled through manual documents or separate spreadsheets. This fragmentation creates data inconsistency, duplicate effort, delayed reporting, and weak transparency.
[Normal] The second major problem is communication. Students, teachers, parents, and administrators need a common platform for schedules, results, announcements, messages, and academic feedback. Without such a platform, updates may be delayed or lost. Educational technology literature identifies communication and timely feedback as important components of effective digital learning environments (DeLone & McLean, 2003; Venkatesh et al., 2003).
[Normal] The third problem is the lack of data-driven academic monitoring. The proposal highlights AI-powered analytics and student performance prediction. Although the current implementation focuses mainly on management and reporting, its data model creates the foundation for later AI modules. Learning analytics research shows that student records, performance data, attendance, and interaction data can support early risk detection and personalized learning support when collected systematically (Delen, 2010; Ifenthaler & Yau, 2020).
[Normal] The gap, therefore, is not only the absence of a software product. The real gap is the absence of an integrated workflow where academic data, financial data, communication, and analytics are stored and processed together. NAMS is proposed to close this gap by connecting stakeholders, modules, and reports in one controlled system.

[Heading 2] 2.1 Concepts and Definitions
[Normal] This literature review focuses on concepts that directly support the proposal and implementation of NAMS. An academy management system is treated as an educational information system, not merely as a website. It integrates operational data, academic records, communication, finance, and reporting. The DeLone and McLean model is relevant because it evaluates information systems through system quality, information quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits (DeLone & McLean, 2003).
[Normal] Learning analytics and educational data mining are relevant because the proposal includes AI-based analytics and performance monitoring. Aldowah et al. (2019) and Romero and Ventura (2020) show that educational data can support decision-making, risk detection, and learning improvement when it is collected and analyzed systematically. For NAMS, this means that student attendance, grades, payments, and timetable data should be stored in a way that supports future analysis.
[Normal] Requirements engineering is the process of discovering, documenting, validating, and managing stakeholder needs. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 is used as a standard reference because it emphasizes clear, testable, necessary, and traceable requirements. In NAMS, requirements are traced from the proposal, questionnaire, and workflow problems to implemented modules such as Students, Payments, Payroll, Reports, Dashboard, and Role Management (International Organization for Standardization et al., 2018).
[Normal] Software quality is evaluated using ISO/IEC 25010:2011, which includes functional suitability, usability, security, performance efficiency, reliability, maintainability, compatibility, and portability. This standard is important because NAMS handles sensitive academic and financial data, so quality must include security and maintainability in addition to visible features (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2011).
[Normal] The Technology Acceptance Model is used because the system must be accepted by real users, not only completed technically. Davis (1989) explains that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use influence acceptance. This is why the questionnaire measures ease of use, system necessity, speed, interface simplicity, mobile access, and future willingness to use the system.

[Heading 2] 2.3 Recent Trends and Technologies
[Normal] A major trend in educational technology is the movement from isolated digital tools to integrated smart education systems. Such systems combine data collection, dashboards, analytics, communication, and personalization. Learning analytics literature shows that institutions can use student data for monitoring progress and identifying academic risk (Ifenthaler & Yau, 2020; Siemens, 2013). The proposal’s AI direction is therefore consistent with recent educational technology trends.
[Normal] The MERN-style technology stack is also consistent with modern scalable web development. Node.js and Express.js support asynchronous API development, MongoDB supports flexible document storage, and React supports interactive user interfaces. These tools match the proposal’s objective of building a scalable, maintainable, and responsive academy platform. From an architecture viewpoint, separating the client, API, data, and security layers improves maintainability and modifiability (Bass et al., 2012).
[Normal] Agile and SDLC hybrid development is another relevant trend. Agile supports feedback, iteration, and adaptive planning, while SDLC preserves structured documentation, requirement analysis, design, testing, deployment, and maintenance. Dikert et al. (2016) show that agile transformations require clear coordination and disciplined practices. For a two-person student team, combining Agile Scrum practices with SDLC phases provides both flexibility and academic documentation discipline.
[Normal] In NAMS, these technologies are not selected only because they are popular. Node.js and Express are suitable because the system requires many API endpoints and middleware for authentication, validation, permissions, and reporting. MongoDB is suitable because academy data contains structured relationships but also flexible fields such as permissions, messages, notifications, and audit histories. React is suitable because administrative dashboards require dynamic components, charts, forms, and role-specific navigation. Flutter is included in the proposal as the mobile application direction because students and teachers need schedule, notification, and result access outside desktop environments.

[Heading 2] 2.7 Financial Transparency and Educational Administration
[Normal] Financial transparency is one of the strongest practical reasons for developing NAMS. In many academies, fee payments, teacher salaries, expenses, and tax-related values are calculated separately. This separation creates risk: a student may be shown as unpaid in one document and paid in another; a teacher salary may be calculated manually without a clear base; financial reports may not match actual payment records. Information systems literature emphasizes that high-quality information should be accurate, timely, relevant, and accessible to authorized users (DeLone & McLean, 2003).
[Normal] The implemented project responds to this problem by linking student payments to the finance module, teacher salary, payroll records, and academy financial reports. This is especially important because the user requested a scenario with 100 students, 10 teachers, 10 students per teacher, 1,000 AF per student, and random teacher salary percentages between 20% and 40%. That scenario is not only test data; it demonstrates that the financial model can be verified mathematically.

[Heading 2] 2.8 Security, Privacy, and Role-Based Access Control
[Normal] NAMS stores sensitive data: student identity, family contact, grades, attendance, payment records, salary values, tax deductions, user accounts, and internal messages. Because of this sensitivity, security must be included in the literature review and not only in implementation. ISO/IEC 25010 includes security as a software quality characteristic, and this supports the project decision to use authentication, authorization, permissions, and protected routes (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2011).
[Normal] Role-based access control is appropriate because different users require different visibility. A student should view personal information and relevant academic records. A teacher should view assigned students and teaching-related records. An administrator should manage records but may not need full system configuration. A super administrator should manage users, roles, permissions, and global reports. This design also supports the principle of least privilege, meaning users should only receive access required for their work.

[Heading 2] 4.2 Architectural Design
[Normal] NAMS uses a layered web architecture. The presentation layer is built with React.js and Vite. The API layer uses Node.js and Express.js. The data layer uses MongoDB with Mongoose schemas. The security layer uses JWT authentication, RBAC, permission keys, and protected routes. The reporting layer includes dashboard analytics, finance aggregation, payroll reports, and academy financial reports. This architecture supports scalability and maintainability as recommended in software architecture literature (Bass et al., 2012).
[Normal] Figure 4.1: Layered Architecture of NAMS
[Normal] The architecture diagram is shown after introducing the design layers. It visually explains how the client, API, database, security, and reporting layers communicate.
[Normal] After examining the architecture, the reason for technology separation becomes clear. The frontend focuses on user interaction. The backend protects business logic and permissions. The database stores institutional records. The reporting layer transforms stored data into management information. This separation supports maintainability because each layer can be modified without rewriting the entire system.

[Heading 2] 4.3 Database Design
[Normal] The database design is based on MongoDB collections represented conceptually through an ERD. Although MongoDB is a document database, the system uses references to preserve relationships among core entities. Student records reference class, subject, teacher, and family. Payment records reference student, teacher, class, subject, and salary records. SalaryRecord stores gross salary, tax, net salary, month, and payment status. This supports both operational transactions and management reports (Connolly & Begg, 2015).
[Normal] Figure 4.2: Entity-Relationship Diagram
[Normal] The ERD is placed after the database design explanation so that the reader can connect the text with the entities and relationships.
[Normal] The ERD shows that the database is designed around relationships that matter to academy workflows. A student belongs to a class and subject, is assigned to a teacher, participates in attendance and exams, and creates fee payment records. Payments support financial reports and teacher salary. Salary records connect payroll and tax. This means the database is not only for storage; it is the foundation of operational automation.

[Heading 2] 4.5 Detailed Design
[Heading 3] 4.5.1 UI/UX Design
[Normal] The UI/UX design focuses on simplicity, role-specific navigation, multilingual labels, light/dark mode readability, and dashboard-based access. Dashboard cards are clickable and route users to their related modules. This decision reduces navigation effort and supports perceived ease of use, which is important for user acceptance (Davis, 1989; Nielsen, 1994).
[Heading 3] 4.5.2 Algorithm Design
[Normal] The most important algorithmic logic is teacher salary calculation. For each teacher, the system identifies assigned students, sums the student fee payments in the selected month, applies the teacher’s commission percentage, calculates gross salary, applies government salary tax, and returns net payable salary.
[Normal] Pseudocode: totalFee = sum(payments of assigned students); grossSalary = totalFee × commissionPercentage / 100; taxAmount = calculateAfghanistanSalaryTax(grossSalary); netSalary = grossSalary - taxAmount;
[Normal] The complexity of this calculation depends mainly on the number of assigned students and payments in the period. With indexing on studentId, teacherId, and paymentDate, the query remains efficient for academy-scale data.
[Heading 2] 4.6 Security Design
[Normal] Security is designed through JWT-based authentication, role-based access control, backend permission middleware, frontend protected routes, and validation of user input. Sensitive data includes student profiles, salary records, tax calculations, payment information, and user credentials. The system follows the principle of least privilege: each role sees only the sections required for its responsibility.
[Heading 2] 4.7 Testing Strategy
[Normal] Testing includes unit tests, integration tests, system tests, and User Acceptance Testing. Unit tests focus on salary calculation, tax calculation, timetable validation, and permission policies. Integration tests focus on API workflows such as student creation, payment recording, salary calculation, and dashboard loading. UAT focuses on whether administrators, teachers, students, and parents can complete their expected workflows without confusion.
