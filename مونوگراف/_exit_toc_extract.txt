CHAPTER 1: INTRODUCTION
This chapter introduces the project and the context in which it was
developed.
It begins by explaining the Afghan Medical Exit Exam and
the preparation challenges faced by its candidates, then presents the problem
statement, objectives, corresponding research questions, scope, limitations, and
significance of the project. It also summarizes the research and development
methodologies, states the project outcomes, and outlines the organization of
the thesis. Together, these sections establish the purpose, direction, boundaries,
and expected contribution of an offline-capable and exam-oriented mobile
preparation application.
1.1
Background
In Afghanistan, medical graduates do not move from graduation into professional
practice through the university degree alone. They must also pass a national
Exit Exam before they can obtain a medical practice permit and register as
practitioners. According to Rahimi et al. (2026), the current official name of this
exam is the National Medical Licensing Examination (NMLE), although it is still
widely referred to in public usage as the Exit Exam. It functions as a licensing
requirement for curative medicine and stomatology graduates who intend to
work in the country, including graduates from local and foreign institutions.
The same review explained that the exam was initially known as the Exit Exam,
was later renamed the Afghanistan Medical Licensing Examination (AMLE),
and then became known as the NMLE. The exam has been reported as a broad
multiple-choice assessment with 160 questions, 360 total marks, a passing
score of 216 (60%), and a four-hour time limit. Its content is divided between
paraclinical subjects, which make up 30% of the exam, and clinical subjects,
which make up 70%. The paraclinical part covers the same broad subjects
for both curative medicine and stomatology, including anatomy, physiology,
pathology, pharmacology, microbiology, and medical ethics, although the exact
content can differ according to the medical or dental focus of the exam. For
curative medicine, the clinical part includes general internal medicine, surgery,
obstetrics and gynecology, pediatrics, ENT (ear, nose, and throat), public
health, and neurology and mental disorders. For stomatology, the clinical
part includes general stomatology, periodontology, pediatric stomatology, oral
and maxillofacial surgery, orthodontics, prosthodontics, and public health
(Afghanistan Medical Council, 2022).
The administration of the medical Exit Exam has changed over time. As
described by Rahimi et al. (2026), the Afghanistan Medical Council (AMC)

It is important to place the Exit Exam within the wider Afghan health examination
landscape. The medical Exit Exam or NMLE is not the same as the health-
science institute examinations taken by graduates of institute-level programs
such as pharmacy, nursing, medical technology, anesthesia, and other related
programs. It is also different from postgraduate specialization and residency
examinations, which are connected to entry into, or completion of, specialty
training after medical graduation. According to Rahimi et al. (2026), health-
science institute examinations and postgraduate specialty pathways have different
candidate groups, purposes, and administrative arrangements. Therefore, this
project focused specifically on the licensing exam for curative medicine and
stomatology graduates, not on institute-level health examinations or postgraduate
specialization exams.
The structure of the Exit Exam makes it different from an ordinary semester test.
It asks graduates to review a large part of their medical training and to perform
under national, timed, high-stakes conditions. In principle, such an exam can
support quality assurance by setting a common professional threshold across
public and private institutions. In practice, preparation is difficult because
Afghan medical education still faces serious resource and quality challenges. A
recent commentary on medical education in Afghanistan reported weaknesses
in infrastructure, learning environment, faculty capacity, access to updated
educational resources, technology use, and financing (Stanikzai et al., 2025).
The same paper noted that many programs still depended heavily on lecture
notes and that available educational resources were not always aligned with
Exit Exam requirements, leaving students to prepare for the exam largely on
their own (Stanikzai et al., 2025).
Student-level evidence pointed in the same direction. In a cross-sectional study
of senior Afghan medical students, only about one fifth of respondents rated their
medical training as excellent, while many reported concerns related to session

organization, faculty support, and access to textbooks and journals (Stanikzai
et al., 2023). These issues matter for Exit Exam preparation because the exam
depends not only on remembering isolated facts, but also on regular revision,
broad subject coverage, and confidence with medical multiple-choice questions.
According to Parker (2020), repeated low-stakes quizzes can support long-term

The Exit 360 app was proposed in response to this preparation need. The
project focused on a mobile-first application that helps medical graduates study
through organized question practice, timed mock tests, feedback, subject-wise
revision, and progress tracking, with online weekly tests, leaderboards, and
peer challenges available when internet access allows. It did not claim to solve
the wider problems of medical education in Afghanistan. Instead, it addressed
a smaller but practical gap by giving graduates a structured, accessible, and
exam-oriented way to prepare for a national licensing exam that covers many
subjects and has direct consequences for their professional future.
1.2
Problem Statement
According to Zhang et al. (2023), practice exams have been shown to improve
exam preparation by helping learners discover knowledge gaps, practice actively,
and retain material for a longer period. As emphasized by Parker (2020),
repeated low-stakes testing has also been valuable, especially when learners
have been able to try questions more than once and use feedback to guide further
study. These findings were relevant for Exit Exam preparation because the
exam has required broad, timed, multiple-choice practice across paraclinical
and clinical subjects.
The main problem has been that many Afghan medical graduates have been
expected to prepare for this licensing exam without enough structured, exam-
oriented support. As described by Rahimi et al. (2026), the Exit Exam has
been described as a national licensing assessment that covers a wide range
of medical subjects and has had to be passed before graduates could obtain
a medical practice permit. Therefore, preparation problems have not only
affected exam performance. Unsuccessful or delayed success has also had
the potential to postpone a graduate’s entry into formal medical practice and
delay the professional income expected after many years of study. A graduate

preparing for such an exam has needed more than general reading. They have
needed a clear way to revise subject areas, attempt timed questions, review
mistakes, and understand whether their preparation has been improving.
However, the available preparation environment has not fully supported this
kind of study. According to Stanikzai et al. (2025), many medical programs in
Afghanistan have had limited access to updated learning resources and have
made weak use of educational technology. The same source also reported
that available learning materials have not always been aligned with Exit Exam
requirements. This has left graduates to connect the exam blueprint, textbooks,
lecture notes, and practice questions mostly by themselves. In a separate study
by Stanikzai et al. (2023), senior medical students reported concerns about
session organization, faculty support, and access to textbooks and journals.
These issues have made preparation feel scattered, especially when graduates
have needed regular practice and timely feedback rather than only passive
review.
Online learning has appeared to offer a useful path by reducing dependence
on fixed class schedules and physical preparation centers. It has also made
question practice, revision, and feedback more flexible for graduates who have
had to study at different times and in different places. Still, a purely online
solution has not been enough in the Afghan context. In a study by Gawhari et al.
(2021), online education in Afghanistan was found to be affected by limited
internet access, slow connection speed, high internet costs, and low availability
of quality digital teaching materials.
Therefore, Exit Exam preparation has needed a solution that is structured but
also realistic for local conditions. Graduates have needed access to subject-wise
questions, timed mock tests, progress tracking, and feedback in a format that
could continue working even when the internet was limited. At the same time,
an internet connection could add further value through online weekly tests,
leaderboards, and peer challenges that have encouraged regular practice. This
has created the need for an offline-capable, mobile-first Exit Exam preparation
application that would support steady practice while also offering online
competitive features when connectivity was available.

1.5
Scope and Limitations
This section defines what was included in the project and what was left outside
its boundaries. The scope clarifies the main features, platforms, users, and data
covered by the system, while the limitations describe the contextual, content,
technical, and resource constraints that shaped the project.
1.5.1
Scope (Inclusions)
Features: The mobile application included a local question bank, offline
subject-wise practice tests, general mixed practice tests, optional timed sessions,
automatic grading, answer review, attempt history, and basic performance
statistics. These features support repeated practice, progress awareness, and
preparation across the clinical and paraclinical subjects of the Exit Exam.
The online scope included authenticated user accounts, backend-managed
weekly tests, leaderboard snapshots, peer or friend challenge workflows, secure
server-side grading and basic notification support. The web admin panel included
controlled tools for reviewing platform activity, inspecting question-bank
summaries, planning weekly tests, publishing leaderboards, and triggering
protected backend operations.
Platforms and technologies: The system was designed as an offline-capable
Android application developed with Flutter. Local SQLite storage was included
for the bundled question bank and offline attempt records, so that the main
practice workflow can remain usable without an internet connection. The online
side used Firebase services for account-based and competitive workflows. The
backend scope included Firebase Authentication, Cloud Firestore, TypeScript
Firebase Cloud Functions, and Firebase Cloud Messaging.
The internal
admin panel intended for controlled operational work was developed as a web
application using React, TypeScript, and Vite.
Users: The target users are curative medicine and stomatology graduates
preparing for the Afghan medical Exit Exam, as well as final-year students
approaching the licensing stage.
Data: The data scope included question-bank content, local and online test
attempts, performance statistics, user profile data, friendship and peer-interaction
records, server-graded submissions, weekly test records, leaderboard records,
challenge records, admin workflow data, and questionnaire responses used for
requirements elicitation.

1.5.2
Limitations (Exclusions)
Context: The project focused on the Afghan medical Exit Exam context and did
not aim to cover all health-sector examinations in Afghanistan. Health-science
institute examinations, specialization or residency examinations, and other
professional tests are outside the direct scope.
The questionnaire used a
limited sample of relevant respondents, so its findings should be treated as
requirements-oriented evidence rather than a complete national measurement
of all Exit Exam candidates.
Content: The question bank used in the project was limited in language and
coverage, with the current content prepared in Dari. A complete, expert-verified,
bilingual Dari and Pashto question bank is outside the current capstone scope
because it requires medical expert review, careful content authorization, and
long-term content maintenance. The system architecture supported future
question-bank expansion, but the present work does not claim to provide a full
official replacement for medical textbooks, preparation centers, or authorized
exam materials.
Technical: Several advanced technical features were also excluded from the
current scope. These include real-time challenge sessions, continuously updating
live leaderboards, large-scale tournaments, advanced adaptive learning, video
lessons, medical animations, real-time chat, and full production deployment
to app stores. Challenge tests were treated as asynchronous workflows, and
leaderboard results were published as backend-generated snapshots after grading
to keep backend reads, writes, and operating costs manageable within the
prototype scope.
Resource: The project was constrained by the capstone timeline, limited
team size, Firebase usage costs, limited formal effectiveness testing, and the
need to validate the system within a prototype environment. Therefore, the
project focused on functional correctness, usability, offline access, secure
backend-controlled online workflows, and preparation support rather than
proving long-term improvement in official Exit Exam pass rates.

Research Methodology
This study used a quantitative research methodology to support requirements
elicitation for an Afghan medical Exit Exam preparation mobile application.
Before preparing the questionnaire, an initial feature set was derived from the
literature review and the analysis of existing local and foreign medical exam
preparation systems. This helped identify relevant preparation features such as
question banks, timed practice tests, answer review, progress tracking, offline
access, and online learning activities.
The main data collection instrument was a structured questionnaire administered
to medical graduates and final-year students preparing for the Afghan medical
Exit Exam. The questionnaire collected feedback about preparation habits,
access to practice tests, internet availability, offline-access needs, feature
usefulness, feature priorities, and expected impact. Although Cochran’s formula
indicated that a larger sample would be required for a statistically representative
survey, the collected responses were treated as requirements-oriented evidence
rather than nationwide statistics.
The questionnaire responses were analyzed using descriptive statistics such
as frequencies, percentages, mean scores, and weighted priority rankings.
The results helped summarize the main preparation problems faced by Exit
Exam candidates and identify which proposed features were considered most
useful. These findings were then used to refine requirements in areas such as
offline question practice, timed mock tests, answer review, progress tracking,
synchronization, and optional online competition.

2.1
Related Work and Existing Systems
Medical exam preparation apps have become an important part of contemporary
test preparation worldwide. Therefore, this section reviewed existing digital
systems for medical licensing and high-stakes examination preparation in order
to position Exit 360 within both the Afghan Exit Exam context and the broader
landscape of exam-preparation technologies. Since the Afghan Exit Exam is
a national licensing requirement that affects graduates’ registration and entry
into formal medical practice (Rahimi et al., 2026), the review first considered
locally relevant Afghan medical exam preparation applications rather than
beginning with general international platforms. The purpose of the review was
not simply to list available applications, but to examine how existing systems
support core preparation needs such as access to question banks, timed practice,
automatic grading, feedback, progress monitoring, offline use, synchronization,
and competitive learning. After the local systems were examined, selected
comparable medical exam preparation platforms from other contexts were
discussed only where their mechanisms provide useful design insight for Exit
360. This analytical approach helped identify the practical and technical gaps
that remain in current solutions, especially for Afghan medical graduates who
may face limited connectivity, unequal access to preparation resources, and
limited structured support while preparing for the national licensing exam.

2.2
Recent Trends and Technologies
Recent developments in educational technology show a clear shift from static
digital content toward interactive, data-informed, and mobile-first learning
systems. In the context of high-stakes licensing examinations, this shift is
especially important because candidates do not only need access to reading
material; they also need repeated practice, feedback, progress awareness,
motivation, and a clear way to organize their preparation. Exit 360 was therefore
connected to several related trends: mobile self-regulated learning, offline-first
design, cloud-backed synchronization, learning analytics, gamified competition,
and cross-platform development. These trends are not independent from one
another. In a preparation system, they work together: mobile access allows
frequent practice, offline storage protects continuity, cloud services support
shared records and competition, and analytics convert attempts into practical
feedback.
One of the most relevant trends is mobile learning, particularly through quiz
and practice-test applications. Mobile devices allow learners to practice outside
fixed classroom schedules, which is valuable for students who cannot regularly
attend preparation centers or who need flexible study time. According to R¨uth
et al. (2021), mobile quiz applications can support self-regulated learning by
giving learners flexibility in terms of location, time, and device. Similarly,
according to Lazarinis et al. (2017), mobile self-assessment allows learners
to test themselves at their own pace and receive feedback without depending
on a formal classroom session. For Exit 360, this trend directly supported the
decision to build an Android-focused mobile application rather than only a
web-based system. Medical graduates preparing for the Exit Exam often need
short, repeated practice sessions across many subjects, and mobile access makes
this more realistic than a system tied to a specific place or schedule.
The educational value of repeated testing is another important foundation for
modern exam preparation systems. Research on practice exams shows that
testing is not only a way to measure knowledge, but also a learning activity in
itself. As argued by Parker (2020), repeated low-stakes quizzes can support
long-term learning more effectively than a single high-stakes assessment, while
according to Zhang et al. (2023), realistic and earlier practice exams can
influence students’ metacognition, study behavior, and exam performance. In
the Exit Exam context, this supports the use of timed subject tests, general
mock tests, and weekly exams. A preparation application should not simply
store past questions; it should create conditions where students can repeatedly
practice under exam-like constraints, receive quick feedback, and adjust their

study behavior over time.
Because Exit 360 was designed for Afghan medical graduates, offline-first
architecture is a particularly important technological trend. Online learning in
Afghanistan faces practical barriers such as limited connectivity, slow internet
speed, high internet costs, and uneven availability of quality digital learning
resources (Gawhari et al., 2021). A purely online preparation system may
therefore fail exactly when students need it most. Offline-first design responds
to this problem by placing essential learning resources on the device and
treating the network as an enhancement rather than a permanent requirement.
In Exit 360, this meant storing a prebundled question bank locally and allowing
students to take subject-wise and general tests even without internet access.
SQLite is suitable for this kind of embedded local storage because it is a small,
self-contained, full-featured SQL database engine widely used in mobile and
desktop applications (SQLite Consortium, 2026). This supports the system’s
need for fast local access to questions, options, answers, and attempt records.
At the same time, offline-only systems can become limited if users cannot
preserve records, compare performance, or participate in shared activities.
For this reason, another recent trend is the combination of local storage with
cloud-backed synchronization.
Cloud services allow a system to manage
authenticated accounts, shared tests, remote grading, leaderboards, and
cross-device or restored-connectivity workflows. Firestore documentation,
for example, describes offline persistence in which actively used data can be
cached locally and local changes can be synchronized with the backend when
the device comes back online (Google Firebase, 2026). Although Exit 360
used its own project-specific data model and synchronization rules, the same
general principle is important: students should be able to continue learning
offline while the system later uploads attempts, updates online records, and
participates in competitive workflows when internet access becomes available.
This is especially relevant for weekly tests and challenge submissions, where
authoritative server-side records are needed for fairness and consistency.
Learning analytics is another major trend in educational applications. Instead
of only telling a student whether a single answer is correct or wrong, analytics
can summarize performance across attempts, subjects, time periods, and test
types. At the application level, Exit 360 applied this idea by tracking individual
attempts, scores, and progress indicators. Such analytics help students see
whether they are improving, which subjects require more work, and how their
performance changes across practice sessions. This addresses a limitation
observed in many existing local apps, where feedback often stops at the number

Gamification and competitive learning have also become common in
exam-preparation systems.
The purpose of gamification in this context
is not entertainment for its own sake, but motivation, engagement, and
repeated participation. Leaderboards are one of the most common competitive
mechanisms. In a systematic review by Li et al. (2024), leaderboards in higher
education were found to positively influence learning motivation, engagement,
and performance.
According to Abdul Halim et al. (2022), leaderboard
gamification can also improve participation and encourage extra learning
activity. For Exit 360, leaderboards are relevant because Exit Exam preparation
is also high-stakes and competitive; students often want to know how their
performance compares with others. However, the implementation must match
the local context. Instead of real-time leaderboard streaming, which may increase
bandwidth and backend cost, Exit 360 used snapshot-based leaderboard updates
for weekly tests. This preserved the motivational value of ranking while keeping
the system more practical for low-connectivity environments.
Related to gamification is the trend of peer-based and challenge-based learning.
Competition can increase effort when it is structured in a healthy way.
According to Ma et al. (2024), competitive attitude and a supportive competitive
environment can be associated with stronger task motivation and performance.
In Exit 360, this idea was implemented through the asynchronous challenge
workflow, where students can challenge followers without needing a real-time
session. This design is important because real-time multiplayer testing would
be more difficult under unstable internet conditions. An asynchronous model
allows students to compete, compare results, and remain socially engaged while
still respecting the connectivity constraints identified in the Afghan educational
context.
Finally, cross-platform and cloud-integrated development tools have made it
more feasible for small student teams to build sophisticated educational systems
within limited time and resource constraints. Flutter is relevant in this regard
because it is a Google-supported framework for building applications across
multiple screens and platforms from a shared codebase (Google, 2025). For
Exit 360, Flutter supported the development of a mobile-first interface while
leaving room for future expansion. Combined with local SQLite storage and
cloud-backed services, this technology stack supported the main design goal of
the project: an application that remains useful offline, becomes richer when
online, and connects practice, review, competition, and analytics in one coherent
workflow. Overall, the recent trends most relevant to Exit 360 are not simply

2.3
Literature Review Synthesis
The reviewed local applications showed that mobile preparation for the Afghan
Exit Exam had already started to emerge, but the local landscape remained early
and incomplete. MedQuiz demonstrated local interest in digital question packs
for the Exit Exam and other health-science examinations, but its online-first
access model, limited visible demo content, paid bank-based structure, and
uncertain public evidence about question-bank depth left important gaps for
students who need dependable daily practice (Azmoonak Tebi, 2026). EXIT
Medical Exam Prep provided a stronger offline model with Dari and Pashto
questions, timed tests, answer review, and two complete Exit papers, but
it remained limited in long-term progress tracking, online competition, and
broader Exit-specific coverage (Zahid apps store, 2026). These two systems
were valuable signs of a growing local app landscape, but they did not fully
address the need for a focused, transparent, offline-capable, and progress-aware
Exit Exam preparation system.
The foreign systems reviewed in this chapter showed a more mature direction
for medical licensing exam preparation. UWorld treated practice as a full cycle
of timed questions, explanation, review, assessment, and weak-area awareness
(UWorld, 2026). AMBOSS connected question practice with a larger medical
knowledge base and performance signals (AMBOSS, 2026), while SMLE Gate
showed how a national licensing-exam platform could focus on simulated exams,
saved questions, notes, result review, and performance tracking (SMLE Gate,
2026). However, these systems were designed for other countries, languages,
examination bodies, and market conditions. Their models often assume larger
content teams, stronger subscription ecosystems, wider platform support, and
more stable internet access than many Afghan graduates can reliably depend on.
The technology and learning trends discussed in this chapter also point to a
specific design gap. According to R¨uth et al. (2021), mobile quiz tools can
support flexible, self-regulated study, and according to Lazarinis et al. (2017),
mobile self-assessment allows learners to practice and receive feedback outside
a formal classroom. As discussed by Parker (2020), repeated quizzes can
support long-term learning, while according to Zhang et al. (2023), realistic
practice exams can support students’ study behavior and exam readiness. At
the same time, Afghan online learning conditions made offline-first design
important (Gawhari et al., 2021). Therefore, the gap was not simply the absence

of question apps. The stronger gap was the absence of an Exit Exam preparation
system that combined exam-aligned practice, offline continuity, timed mock
testing, answer review, progress analytics, and optional online competition in
one coherent workflow.
Exit 360 was designed to respond to this gap by adapting the practical lessons
from both local and foreign systems while remaining focused on the Afghan
Exit Exam context. Its offline layer was intended to support subject-wise and
general tests through a prebundled local question bank. Its online layer added
authenticated profiles, synchronization, weekly tests, leaderboard snapshots,
and asynchronous challenges when internet access was available. Its analytics

of only seeing the result of one test. In this way, the project did not claim
that no Exit Exam preparation applications exist. Rather, it addressed the
remaining gap between isolated question-practice tools and a more complete,
offline-capable preparation system suited to Afghan medical graduates’ learning
and connectivity realities.

CHAPTER 3: METHODOLOGY
This chapter explains the research and development methods used to design
and implement Exit 360. It describes the quantitative questionnaire-based
requirements elicitation process, the target population and sampling approach,
data collection and analysis, system requirements, and the main development
methodology followed during the project. It also presents the system design,
architecture, database design, implementation approach, and testing activities
that connect the identified preparation needs with the final mobile application.
3.1
Research Methodology
This study used a quantitative research methodology to support the requirements
elicitation for Exit 360. The main data collection instrument was a structured
questionnaire administered to medical graduates and final-year students
preparing for the Afghan Medical Exit Exam. It was used to measure candidates’
preparation habits, access challenges, internet availability, feature priorities,
and expected usefulness of an Exit Exam preparation mobile application.
Before preparing the questionnaire, an initial feature set was derived from the
literature review and analysis of existing medical exam preparation systems both
local and foreign. The reviewed literature helped identify the educational value
of repeated practice, timed tests, feedback, and progress monitoring, while the
existing-system review showed how local and international preparation apps
support question banks, mock tests, answer review, offline access, analytics, and
online learning features. These sources were used to create a preliminary list of
features for Exit 360, which was then presented to the target users through the
questionnaire.
The quantitative questionnaire responses were analyzed using descriptive
statistics such as frequencies, percentages, mean scores, and priority rankings.
This made it possible to summarize the main preparation problems faced by Exit
Exam candidates and to identify which proposed features were considered most
useful. The findings were then used to refine the functional and non-functional
requirements of Exit 360, especially in areas such as offline question practice,
timed mock tests, answer review, progress tracking, synchronization, and
optional online competition.

Requirements elicitation began by translating the problem context into a practical
feature list for Exit 360. The literature review showed that Exit Exam candidates

3.1.3
Data Collection
Data for the user requirements part of the study were collected through a

directly relevant to Exit Exam preparation within the limited project timeline. It
also matched the purpose of this part of the research, which was to understand
candidates’ preparation conditions, their access to practice tests and internet,
and their level of interest in the proposed features of Exit 360.
Two versions of the questionnaire were prepared. The English version was
prepared for thesis documentation, while the Persian version was prepared
for actual respondents so that the questions would be easier to understand
and answer. Data collection was carried out through printed Persian copies
distributed in person to eligible respondents. This approach was selected
because the target respondents were more reachable through direct distribution,
and it reduced the need for respondents to depend on an internet connection
while completing the survey.

introduction explaining that the survey was part of a Kabul University Software
Engineering capstone project for developing an Exit Exam preparation mobile
application. The introduction also informed respondents that participation was
voluntary, that the survey was anonymous, and that no personal identifying
information would be published.

respondents. It asked about age, gender, original province, current study level,
whether the respondent had already taken the Afghan Medical Exit Exam,

or coaching program. These items helped describe the respondent group and
provided context for interpreting their answers.
