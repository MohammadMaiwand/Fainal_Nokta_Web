[Heading 1] Abstract
[Normal] This monograph presents the analysis, design, implementation, and evaluation of the Nokta Academy Management System, a full-stack educational management platform designed to digitize academic, administrative, communication, and financial operations in an academy environment. The system contains modules for student registration, teacher assignment, classes, subjects, timetable, attendance, examinations, results, student fee payments, payroll, salary tax calculation, academy financial reporting, notifications, messaging, role management, audit logging, and dashboard analytics. The implementation uses a React and Vite frontend, an Express and TypeScript API, and a MongoDB database accessed through Mongoose.
[Normal] The study follows an applied mixed-methods approach. Requirements were elicited through workflow analysis, stakeholder role analysis, and a structured questionnaire. The population was defined as 100 academy stakeholders, and 75 valid responses were selected for analysis. Among these respondents, 64 supported the project, resulting in an approval rate of 85.33%. The development approach was iterative and incremental because the system consists of related modules that can be implemented, tested, and improved in stages.
[Normal] The academic foundation of the study is based on software engineering, requirements engineering, design science research, information systems success, technology acceptance, software quality, database design, usability engineering, UML modeling, and software architecture. Requirements engineering is aligned with ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018, quality evaluation is aligned with ISO/IEC 25010:2011, and user evaluation is informed by the Technology Acceptance Model and the DeLone and McLean Information Systems Success Model (Davis, 1989; DeLone & McLean, 2003; International Organization for Standardization et al., 2018; International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2011).

[Heading 1] Chapter 1: Introduction
[Heading 2] 1.1 Background of the Study
[Normal] Academies and private educational institutions manage many operational processes: student registration, teacher assignment, class scheduling, subject allocation, attendance tracking, examination management, result publication, fee payment, salary calculation, tax deduction, messaging, notification, and management reporting. When these processes are performed manually or through disconnected spreadsheets, the academy faces duplicated work, delayed reporting, data inconsistency, weak accountability, and limited visibility into academic and financial performance. The Nokta Academy Management System was developed to address these issues by providing a unified digital platform in which academic, financial, and communication data are connected.
[Normal] Software engineering literature emphasizes that software should be developed from clearly understood requirements, designed through maintainable architecture, and verified against user needs (Pressman & Maxim, 2020; Sommerville, 2016). This principle is important for the Nokta project because the system is not merely a website; it is an operational information system that must support real decisions and daily academy workflows.
[Heading 2] 1.2 Problem Statement
[Normal] The main problem addressed by this project is the absence of an integrated system that can manage academic and financial operations consistently and securely. In a manual environment, student records may be stored separately from payment records, teacher assignments may not be connected to payroll, and reports may require repeated calculations. This creates risks in data accuracy, fee tracking, salary transparency, and administrative decision-making.
[Normal] A practical example is teacher salary calculation. In the updated Nokta system, student fee payments are recorded, each teacher has a commission percentage, salary is calculated from the assigned students’ payments, government salary tax is applied, and net payable salary is displayed during employee payment. Without integration, this process becomes difficult to audit and easy to miscalculate.
[Heading 2] 1.3 Aim and Objectives
[Normal] The aim of this study is to design, implement, and evaluate a full-stack academy management system that improves efficiency, transparency, security, and reporting quality in academy operations.

[Heading 2] 2.2 Educational Management Systems as Information Systems
[Normal] An academy management platform is an organizational information system because it collects data, processes it according to institutional rules, and presents information for planning and decision-making. The value of such a system is not limited to record keeping. Its value appears when data from multiple operational areas are integrated: students are linked with classes and subjects; subjects are linked with teachers; student payments are linked with finance and payroll; and payroll records are linked with tax and salary payment.
[Normal] The DeLone and McLean model explains information system success through system quality, information quality, service quality, use, user satisfaction, and net benefits (DeLone & McLean, 2003). In the Nokta system, system quality is represented by a modular architecture and role-based access; information quality is represented by structured records and reliable calculations; and net benefits are represented by faster reporting, transparent payments, and reduced manual work.
[Heading 2] 2.3 Requirements Engineering and Traceability
[Normal] Requirements engineering provides a disciplined way to transform stakeholder needs into system specifications. ISO/IEC/IEEE 29148:2018 states that requirements should be clear, feasible, verifiable, necessary, and traceable. In the Nokta project, this standard is reflected in the way questionnaire findings and workflow problems are linked to concrete modules such as Students, Payments, Payroll, Reports, and Dashboard (International Organization for Standardization et al., 2018).
[Normal] Traceability is especially important in this project because academic and financial functions are interdependent. For example, the requirement for student fee payment is not isolated; it affects remaining balance, teacher commission salary, financial reports, and dashboard statistics. Therefore, the requirements process was not limited to listing features. It also examined how a change in one module affects other modules.

[Heading 2] 2.4 Iterative and Incremental Development
[Normal] Iterative and incremental development is suitable for systems with multiple modules and evolving stakeholder expectations. Sommerville (2016) explains that incremental development allows useful software components to be delivered and improved over time. Pressman and Maxim (2020) also emphasize that iterative refinement reduces project risk because feedback can be incorporated before the system becomes too rigid.
[Normal] The Nokta project used this approach in practice. The first increments focused on authentication, roles, and the API foundation. Later increments added academic modules such as students, teachers, classes, subjects, timetable, attendance, exams, and results. Financial increments added student payments, expenses, payroll, tax, and academy financial reporting. Usability increments improved the dashboard, module links, light/dark mode display, and entity icons. This sequencing demonstrates why incremental development was more appropriate than a purely linear waterfall model.
[Heading 2] 2.5 Design Science Research Perspective
[Normal] This project can also be understood through design science research. In design science, the researcher builds and evaluates an artifact intended to solve a real organizational problem. Hevner et al. (2004) describe design science as a framework for creating useful information system artifacts, while Wieringa (2014) emphasizes problem investigation, treatment design, and validation. The Nokta system is the artifact, the academy management problem is the context, and the questionnaire plus testing results form part of the evaluation.
[Normal] The design science perspective is useful because the project is not only a theoretical investigation. It produces a working software system. The quality of the research therefore depends on whether the artifact addresses the problem, whether it is designed systematically, and whether it is evaluated using relevant evidence.
[Heading 2] 2.6 Software Architecture and Layered Design
[Normal] Architecture defines the major structural decisions of a software system. Bass et al. (2012) explain that architecture affects quality attributes such as modifiability, performance, security, and maintainability. The Nokta system uses a layered architecture: a client layer, an API layer, a data layer, a security layer, and a reporting layer. This separation was selected because the system must support multiple clients, many API modules, database persistence, role-based access, and managerial reports.
[Normal] The design decision to separate frontend and backend improves maintainability. React components and pages can evolve without changing database models directly, while backend routes and services can enforce business rules independently. The security layer ensures that authorization is checked at both the interface and API levels.

[Heading 2] 2.7 Database Design and ERD Rationale
[Normal] Database design is central to the project because the system relies on relationships among users, students, classes, subjects, payments, salary records, attendance, exams, results, messages, and notifications. Connolly and Begg (2015) argue that database design must support both operational processing and information retrieval. Elmasri and Navathe (2016) similarly emphasize entity relationships, keys, constraints, and data integrity as core database design concerns.
[Normal] MongoDB was selected because the project uses a JavaScript/TypeScript stack and requires flexible but structured documents. Mongoose schemas provide structure, validation, references, and indexes. The ERD represents conceptual relationships even though the physical implementation uses document collections. For example, Student has references to Class, Subject, User as teacher, and Family. Payment can reference Student, Teacher, Subject, Class, and SalaryRecord. SalaryRecord references User and stores gross salary, tax amount, and net salary.

[Heading 2] 2.9 Software Quality and ISO/IEC 25010
[Normal] ISO/IEC 25010:2011 defines product quality characteristics such as functional suitability, performance efficiency, compatibility, usability, reliability, security, maintainability, and portability. These characteristics are relevant to Nokta because the system handles sensitive student and financial records, supports multiple roles, and must remain maintainable as modules grow (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2011).
[Normal] Functional suitability is achieved through modules that match academy workflows. Usability is supported through a role-based dashboard, multilingual interface, light/dark mode, and clickable cards. Security is addressed through JWT authentication, permission keys, route middleware, and protected frontend routes. Maintainability is supported through a modular backend structure and reusable frontend configuration.

[Heading 2] 2.10 User Experience and Technology Acceptance
[Normal] Technology acceptance is important because a system can fail even if it is technically correct. Davis (1989) found that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use strongly influence user acceptance. Venkatesh et al. (2003) extended acceptance theory by integrating factors such as performance expectancy, effort expectancy, social influence, and facilitating conditions. These concepts informed the user evaluation questionnaire, especially questions about system necessity, ease of use, speed, multilingual support, mobile access, and future willingness to use the system.
[Normal] User experience literature also guided interface improvements. Nielsen (1994) emphasizes usability, consistency, error prevention, and efficiency. Garrett (2011) connects user-centered design with information structure and interface design. Rubin and Chisnell (2008) explain how usability testing can reveal practical problems in real interaction. In the Nokta project, improvements such as clickable dashboard sections, readable light/dark mode, clear payroll summaries, and icon-based entity cards reflect these principles.

[Heading 1] Chapter 3: Methodology
[Heading 2] 3.1 Research Methodology
[Normal] The research methodology is applied mixed-methods. It is applied because the study aims to solve a real management problem in an academy. It is mixed-methods because it combines quantitative questionnaire analysis with qualitative workflow and role analysis. Creswell and Creswell (2018) explain that mixed-methods research is appropriate when numeric evidence and contextual explanation are both needed. This project required numeric evidence of user acceptance and qualitative understanding of academy workflows.
[Normal] The research component answers questions such as: What problems do stakeholders face in the current management process? Which features are most important? How necessary is a digital system? Are users willing to adopt it? The design component answers questions such as: Which architecture should be used? How should roles and permissions be structured? Which database entities are needed? How should payroll and tax be calculated?

[Heading 2] 3.2 Population, Sample, and Sampling Formula
[Normal] The population was defined as 100 stakeholders connected to the academy. This population includes students, teachers, administrators, and parents. A sample of 75 respondents was selected because these respondents had direct or indirect involvement with the academy processes. The sample was purposive and accessible rather than random, because the purpose of the study was to collect informed requirements from relevant stakeholders.
[Normal] The project approval rate was calculated with the following formula:
[Normal] Approval Rate = (Number of approvals / Sample size) × 100
[Normal] Approval Rate = (64 / 75) × 100 = 85.33%

[Heading 2] 3.3 Requirements Elicitation Method
[Normal] Requirements elicitation was performed through three methods: questionnaire, role analysis, and workflow analysis. The questionnaire captured user priorities and acceptance indicators. Role analysis identified which users should access which functions. Workflow analysis examined real academy operations such as student registration, fee payment, attendance, result management, teacher salary calculation, tax deduction, and reporting. This combination was selected because a questionnaire alone cannot fully describe system behavior, while workflow analysis alone cannot measure user acceptance.
[Normal] The questionnaire contained 20 items grouped into seven sections: general information, current management status, required features, user experience, information management, current problems, and suggestions. Several items used Likert-type scales from 1 to 5. Likert (1932) introduced this approach for measuring attitudes, and it remains useful for evaluating perceptions such as satisfaction, ease of use, and necessity.

[Heading 2] 3.4 Development Methodology
[Normal] The development methodology was iterative and incremental. This method was selected because Nokta is not a single-feature application; it includes many modules that depend on one another. A linear approach would make it difficult to incorporate user feedback after each module. Iterative development allowed the system to evolve through cycles: build a module, test it, identify issues, and improve it.
[Normal] In the design part of the methodology, iterative development was used to divide the system into manageable modules. Authentication and role management were designed first because other modules depend on user identity and permissions. Academic modules were designed next because students, teachers, classes, and subjects form the base data. Financial modules were then designed because payments and payroll require student and teacher relationships. Reporting modules were designed after operational data became available.
[Normal] In the implementation part of the methodology, the same incremental structure was applied. The backend was organized into modules such as auth, students, teachers, subjects, attendance, payments, payroll, finance, and reports. The frontend used route-based pages and reusable CRUD configuration. This structure made it possible to fix specific problems without rewriting the whole project; for example, the subjects loading error was fixed by aligning backend permission context and frontend visibility logic.

[Heading 2] 3.5 System Design Method
[Normal] The system design method combined layered architecture, role-based access design, database relationship modeling, and user-centered interface design. Layered architecture was used because the system needs separation between client presentation, API logic, database persistence, security, and reporting. This decision was based on software architecture principles that link architecture to maintainability and quality attributes (Bass et al., 2012).
[Normal] Database design was guided by entity relationship analysis. The project uses MongoDB, but the conceptual design still identifies entities, attributes, and relationships. This was necessary because the academy domain has many connected records. For example, Student connects to Class, Subject, Teacher, Family, Payment, Attendance, Exam, and Result. SalaryRecord connects to User and Payment. These relationships were drawn in the ERD to make the structure understandable for both technical and academic review.
[Normal] Use case design was used to clarify system behavior from the user perspective. Each actor has different responsibilities: students view information and payments; teachers manage assigned academic activities; administrators manage operational records; parents view child-related information; and super administrators manage permissions and reports. The use case method was selected because it communicates system scope clearly and supports validation with non-technical stakeholders (Fowler, 2004; Larman, 2004).

[Heading 2] 3.6 Implementation and Testing Method
[Normal] Implementation was performed with a React, Vite, and Tailwind frontend; an Express and TypeScript backend; and a MongoDB database. React Query was used for data fetching and caching, React Router for navigation, and Mongoose for schema-based database access. Joi validation, JWT authentication, permission middleware, and role-based frontend guards were used to reduce invalid data and unauthorized access.
[Normal] Testing was performed at multiple levels. Unit and service tests were used for salary tax, payroll commission, timetable validation, route policy, and error handling. Functional testing was performed by logging in as different roles and verifying that the correct modules appeared. User interface testing was performed through direct interaction with pages such as dashboard, subjects, payments, payroll, and academy financial report. This testing approach reflects the ISO/IEC 25010 quality view because it checks functionality, usability, security, and maintainability concerns (International Organization for Standardization & International Electrotechnical Commission, 2011).

[Heading 1] Chapter 5: Discussion
[Normal] The results show that the Nokta Academy Management System is aligned with stakeholder needs and accepted by the majority of sampled users. The 85.33% approval rate suggests that users recognize the usefulness of digitizing academy operations. This finding is consistent with TAM because users are more likely to accept a system when they perceive it as useful and easy to use (Davis, 1989).
[Normal] The results also align with the DeLone and McLean model. System quality is reflected in the modular architecture and role-based design. Information quality is reflected in connected student, payment, payroll, and report records. Net benefits are reflected in reduced manual work, more transparent salary calculations, and better dashboard-based decision support (DeLone & McLean, 2003).
[Normal] The main limitation is that the questionnaire sample is limited to 75 respondents from one academy context. A broader evaluation across multiple academies would provide stronger generalization. Another limitation is that long-term user satisfaction was not measured after several months of real deployment. Future work should include production monitoring, backup policy, security testing, and extended mobile evaluation.
[Heading 1] Chapter 6: Conclusion
[Normal] This monograph presented a complete study of the Nokta Academy Management System, covering introduction, literature review, methodology, results, discussion, and conclusion. The project addresses a real academy management problem by integrating academic records, financial operations, salary calculation, tax handling, reports, messaging, notifications, and role-based access into a single platform.
[Normal] The study found that 85.33% of the sampled respondents approved the project. The implemented modules match the highest-priority user needs, including registration, grades, attendance, timetable, payments, reports, and notifications. The system also supports advanced financial requirements such as teacher commission salary, government salary tax, and academy financial reporting. Therefore, the project is technically functional, practically useful, and academically grounded.
[Normal] Future development should include long-term user evaluation, stronger security testing, richer report exports, mobile application testing, automated backup and recovery, and advanced analytics for student performance and financial forecasting.
